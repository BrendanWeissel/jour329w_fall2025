## 1. Local Sports Landscape Overview  

### What the Eastern Shore cares about – and why  

The Eastern Shore is a tight‑knit patchwork of small towns, each with its own high‑school mascot, booster club, and a handful of rivalries that have been simmering for generations. The sports that dominate the conversation are:

| Sport | Why it matters | Signature Rivalries / Traditions |
|-------|----------------|---------------------------------|
| **Football** | Friday night lights are a community gathering point; the sport drives year‑round booster fundraising and alumni engagement. | *Accomack vs. Northampton*, *Easton vs. Chincoteague* |
| **Basketball (boys & girls)** | Winter indoor events are the social hub when outdoor fields are off‑season; the “Mid‑Atlantic Classic” tournament draws regional attention. | *Wachapreague vs. Exmore*, *Tidewater vs. Crisfield* |
| **Soccer (boys & girls)** | Growing participation; the “Coastal Cup” brings together all the county schools in a single‑day showcase. | *Salisbury vs. Wicomico* (county‑wide draw) |
| **Baseball / Softball** | Summer leagues and “town‑team” baseball keep the coverage calendar busy into August. | *Easton’s “Old‑Timer” series* |
| **Field Hockey & Lacrosse (girls)** | Strong youth pipelines; both sports are tied to scholarship pipelines for the region’s top athletes. | *St. Mary’s Academy vs. Chincoteague Prep* |
| **Wrestling** | One‑on‑one drama that translates into compelling human‑interest angles; strong community club support. | *Annual “Coast‑to‑Coast” dual meet* |

**Seasonal rhythms** dictate story flow:

| Season | Core Sports | Typical Story Opportunities |
|--------|-------------|------------------------------|
| **Late Summer (August‑Sept)** | Baseball, Softball, Pre‑season Football | Roster cuts, “First‑year” players, community fundraisers |
| **Fall (Oct‑Nov)** | Football, Boys’ & Girls’ Soccer, Field Hockey | Game recaps, rivalry previews, injury updates, “Coach’s Corner” features |
| **Winter (Dec‑Feb)** | Basketball, Wrestling, Indoor Track | Playoff brackets, senior‑night stories, academic‑athlete balance pieces |
| **Spring (Mar‑May)** | Lacrosse, Girls’ Soccer, Baseball/Softball (final stretch) | Tournament coverage, scholarship announcements, off‑season training insights |
| **Summer (June‑July)** | Community leagues, “town‑team” baseball, 7‑on‑7 football | Human‑interest profiles, youth‑development pipelines, booster‑club fund drives |

Understanding these ebbs lets you anticipate when the “big” stories will surface and when you’ll need to pivot to feature work to keep the beat alive.

## 2. Reporter Lifestyle & Workflow  

### A “typical” weekly rhythm (adjust as needed)

| Day | Core Tasks | Timing Tips |
|-----|------------|-------------|
| **Monday** | Review weekend game notes, file quick‑turn recaps (deadline often early Tuesday). Update stats database. | Use a 30‑minute “recap sprint” – bullet notes, then flesh out later. |
| **Tuesday** | Pitch a feature or preview for the coming week; attend morning practice or team meeting. | Keep pitch email under 150 words; attach a one‑sentence “why now” hook. |
| **Wednesday** | Mid‑week deep‑dive – interview a coach/parent, gather quotes for a feature. | Block 1–2 hrs for “in‑the‑field” notes; bring a recorder and a notebook. |
| **Thursday** | Travel to a Friday night game (often >30 mi). Pre‑game notes, quick post‑game recap (if deadline). | Pack a “road kit”: charger, spare batteries, printed schedule, snacks. |
| **Friday** | Cover the night game, post‑game write‑up (deadline often midnight). | Arrive 45 min early for locker‑room access; take a photo of the scoreboard for reference. |
| **Saturday** | Optional “bonus” game, community event, or early‑morning prep for Sunday. | Use Saturday for longer‑form pieces that don’t require same‑day deadline. |
| **Sunday** | Rest, update the master spreadsheet, plan next week’s beats, respond to emails. | Block 1 hr for “admin” – roster updates, contacts, calendar syncing. |

**Late‑night games & long travel:**  
- **Car pool** with a fellow reporter or the school’s media liaison when distances exceed 45 mi.  
- **Stay stocked** on water, portable power, and a small first‑aid kit (you never know when you’ll be the designated driver.); a coffee thermos is a lifesaver for 10 pm games.  
- **Turnaround discipline:** Draft the recap on the road (using a laptop or tablet), then polish at home. Aim to have a clean version **within 2 hours** of game‑end; editors will expect a polished story before the next morning’s edition.

**Contact list hygiene:**  
- Keep a **master Google Sheet** (or Airtable) with columns: Name, Role, School, Phone, Email, Preferred contact method, Last contact date, Notes.  
- Tag each entry (e.g., “Coach‑Football‑Accomack”). Use filters to pull up the right list for a quick email blast.  
- Schedule a **monthly “touch‑base”** (quick 5‑minute call or email) to keep relationships warm; mark it in your calendar.

**Balancing multiple beats during playoffs:**  
- Prioritize **games with playoff implications** or historic rivalries.  
- If two games clash, consider: (1) Which story will generate the most community interest? (2) Which outlet needs the coverage most urgently? (3) Can you send a trusted intern or a “shadow” reporter?  
- Use a **decision matrix** (impact × deadline) to justify coverage choices to editors.

## 3. Building and Maintaining Relationships  

### The “first‑impression” playbook  

1. **Introduce yourself in person** (or via video call if distance is a barrier). Bring a business card, a short “beat sheet” (your name, outlet, sports you cover, publishing schedule).  
2. **Ask for a quick tour** of the facility; coaches love showing off their home field, and it gives you a natural conversation starter.  
3. **Listen more than you speak** – note the coach’s priorities (e.g., “We need to protect our seniors from over‑exposure”) and reflect them in your reporting.

### Consistent, non‑intrusive follow‑up  

- **One‑sentence check‑ins** after a big win or loss (“Congrats on the win! Any thoughts on the next opponent?”).  
- **Quarter‑yearly “pulse” emails** to boosters: “I’m planning a feature on community support – any stories you’d like to share?”  
- Keep a **“follow‑up log”** in your contacts spreadsheet; note the date, topic, and response. This prevents accidental over‑contact.

### Identifying the gatekeepers  

| Role | Why they matter | How to connect |
|------|----------------|----------------|
| **Team manager** (often a parent/volunteer) | Controls locker‑room access, distributes media packets, knows roster nuances. | Offer to help with stats entry after a game; bring a thank‑you note after a big tournament. |
| **Long‑time booster** | Holds historical data, photos, and the “story vault.” | Attend booster meetings, volunteer for fundraising events, ask for archival photos for a feature. |
| **Athletic Director (AD)** | Sets overall policy, approves interviews, knows conference scheduling. | Schedule a brief “beat‑intro” meeting at the start of the school year; bring a printed copy of your editorial calendar. |
| **Assistant/Position coaches** | Provide technical insight (e.g., offensive scheme, player development). | Ask for a quick “breakdown” after a game; follow up with a “thanks for the insight” email that references a specific point they made. |

### Roster maintenance – the evergreen chore  

- **Collect official rosters** at the first team meeting and again after each preseason tryout.  
- Verify spelling with **two sources**: the school’s website and the coach’s media guide.  
- Create a **“Roster Tracker”** spreadsheet (columns: Player, #, Position, Grade, Height, Weight, Hometown, Status – e.g., “redshirt”, “injured”).  
- Update the sheet **every Friday** after games – note any new walk‑ons, transfers, or eligibility changes.  

### Sensitive conversations (injuries, academics, transfers)  

- **Never assume** a player wants their medical details public. Start with “I understand you may not want to discuss specifics; could you share what you’re comfortable with for the story?”  
- **Confidentiality promise:** If a coach tells you a player will be out “for the season,” write the piece with a “source says” tag and give the school a chance to confirm.  
- **Academic issues:** Approach the AD or guidance counselor, not the coach, and respect FERPA guidelines. Offer the student/parents a chance to comment before publishing.

## 4. Reporting and Writing Guidance  

### Story formats you’ll use  

| Type | Core purpose | Typical length | Key ingredients |
|------|--------------|----------------|-----------------|
| **Game recap** | Deliver the result & highlight moments. | 300‑500 words (print) | Final score, scoring summary, key stats, a quote from each coach, a “next game” teaser. |
| **Advance preview** | Build anticipation, set stakes. | 400‑600 words | Records, rival history, injury updates, player to watch, “what to watch for” (scheme). |
| **Feature** | Deep dive on a person, trend, or community angle. | 800‑2,000 words (online) | Narrative arc, multiple sources, vivid scenes, background context. |
| **Trend piece** | Look at a broader pattern (e.g., rise in girls’ lacrosse participation). | 600‑1,200 words | Data points, quotes from experts, historical comparison, visual (chart/graph). |
| **Accountability story** | Examine a policy, funding issue, or controversy. | 800‑1,500 words | Public records, multiple viewpoints, clear timeline, impact on student‑athletes. |
| **Profile** | Humanize a coach, athlete, or community leader. | 1,000‑1,500 words | Life story, motivations, anecdotes, photo portrait, future outlook. |

#### Crafting a strong feature  

1. **Hook:** Open with a vivid, sensory detail (“The smell of fresh-cut grass hung over the field as 3‑year‑old twins sprinted to the sidelines…”).  
2. **Character arc:** Show the subject’s journey—conflict, struggle, triumph. Use **direct quotes** that reveal personality, not just facts.  
3. **Scene work:** Re‑create a moment (a practice drill, a locker‑room pep talk). Let readers hear the coach’s cadence or the crowd’s roar.  
4. **Context:** Weave in stats or historical facts that elevate the story beyond anecdote.  
5. **Resolution:** End with a forward‑looking note—what’s next for the subject, or a broader implication for the community.

#### Fairness & avoiding boosterism  

- **Balance sources:** For every quote from a booster, include at least one perspective from a neutral observer (e.g., an opposing coach, a school administrator, a former player).  
- **Fact‑check every claim** that could affect a program’s reputation (e.g., “Coach X has a 70% graduation rate”).  
- **Separate opinion from reporting**; if you write an editorial, label it clearly.  

#### Common pitfalls in local sports coverage  

- **Over‑reliance on the “coach‑quote.”** Mix in player, parent, and community voices.  
- **“Score‑only” recaps** that lack context—readers want to know *why* a game mattered.  
- **Booster‑centric language** (“our beloved program”)—stay objective.  
- **Cliché phrases** (“brought the house down,” “came out swinging”). Replace with concrete images.

## 5. Stats, Rosters, and Data Management  

### Collecting stats when schools are “DIY”  

1. **Ask for the official box score** (often emailed to the media liaison).  
2. **Cross‑check with the opponent’s box score**; discrepancies usually surface here.  
3. **If no official sheet exists**, attend the game and **track your own stats** using a simple spreadsheet template (time‑stamped rows for each scoring play).  
4. **Confirm with the team manager** after the game—most will happily verify numbers.

### Building your personal database  

- **Tool choice:** Google Sheets for quick sharing, Airtable or Notion for richer relational data (link players to games, injuries, awards).  
- **Core tables:**  
  - *Players* (ID, name, school, class, position, stats per season)  
  - *Games* (date, opponent, location, final score, key moments)  
  - *Season Summary* (standings, playoff results, records)  
- **Automation tip:** Use the school’s public schedule (often a .ics file) to auto‑populate your “Games” table via Zapier or IFTTT.  

### Staying organized across turnover  

- **Season‑end audit:** Export the current season’s roster to a master archive folder labeled “2024‑25 – Football – Rosters.”  
- **Version control:** Keep a “master” file for each sport with a “history” tab that logs every roster change (add/drop, transfer).  
- **Naming convention:** *[Sport]_[School]_[Season]_[DocumentType]* (e.g., *Football_Accomack_2024‑25_Roster.xlsx*).  

### Tracking unofficial record books & rivalry histories  

- **Create a “Rivalry Log”** – a simple table: Rival, First Meeting, Overall Record, Notable Games (year, score, story angle).  
- **Leverage boosters** for old newspaper clippings or photo archives; digitize and store in a shared cloud folder.  
- **Stat alerts:** Set Google Alerts for “Accomack football record” or “Eastern Shore softball championship” to catch any new references that could enrich a story.

## 6. Playoffs, Overlaps & Coverage Strategy  

### The juggling act  

1. **Map every sport’s playoff window** on a master calendar (color‑coded).  
2. **Identify “conflict zones”** – e.g., Friday night football vs. Saturday basketball quarterfinals.  
3. **Apply a decision matrix:**  

| Factor | Weight (1‑5) | Score (1‑5) | Total |
|--------|--------------|-------------|-------|
| Community impact (rivalry, attendance) | 5 | 4 | 20 |
| Editorial deadline urgency | 4 | 5 | 20 |
| Access (locker‑room, media pass) | 3 | 3 | 9 |
| Personal expertise (knowledge of sport) | 2 | 4 | 8 |
| **Total** |   |   | **57** |

   - The highest total wins the slot; the runner‑up can be covered by a colleague or via a “quick recap” from the field.  

### Staying ahead of schedule changes  

- **Subscribe to conference calendars** (email alerts).  
- **Maintain a “Game‑Change Log”** – date, original time, new time, reason (rain, field issues). Update your spreadsheet immediately.  
- **Build relationships with grounds‑keepers and conference schedulers**; they often know about potential changes before an official announcement.  

### Tips for playoff depth  

- **Pre‑game “road‑to‑playoffs” series** – a short 300‑word piece each week leading up to the tournament, summarizing a team’s journey.  
- **Human‑interest “playoff‑prep” profiles** – focus on seniors, family traditions, or a coach’s career milestone.  
- **Post‑game “impact” pieces** – what the win/loss means for the community, scholarship chances, or next‑year recruiting.

## 8. What to Avoid  

### Common rookie missteps  

1. **“Score‑only” recaps** – Readers want the *story* behind the number. Add context, a quote, and a highlight.  
2. **Over‑reliance on a single source** – It creates echo‑chamber bias; diversify your contacts.  
3. **“Booster‑centric” language** – Phrases like “our beloved program” betray objectivity.  
4. **Neglecting proof‑reading** – Small errors (misspelling a player’s name) damage credibility in tight‑knit communities.  

### Ethical traps  

| Trap | Red flag | How to sidestep |
|------|----------|-----------------|
| **Favoritism** | Consistently positive coverage of one school, even when performance lags. | Keep a personal “coverage balance sheet” that logs how many stories you write for each program per month. |
| **Over‑dependence on boosters** | Relying on a booster for stats and quotes without verification. | Cross‑check booster data with official school releases; treat boosters as “source, not source of truth.” |
| **Invasion of privacy** | Publishing medical or disciplinary details without consent. | Follow FERPA, seek written permission from the student/parent, and give the school a chance to review. |
| **Cliché‑heavy prose** | “The crowd went wild,” “He left it all on the field.” | Replace generic adjectives with concrete sensory details (sound, sight, smell). |

### Avoiding stale writing  

- **Swap “the team” for the *team’s nickname*** (e.g., “the Red‑Hawks”) after the first mention.  
- **Rotate descriptive verbs**: *charged, surged, snapped, bulldozed, sliced through.*  
- **Vary sentence length** – a short, punchy line after a dramatic play can heighten tension.  
- **Show, don’t tell** – instead of “the game was intense,” describe the “30‑second clock ticking down with the score tied at 21‑21, the crowd’s collective breath held.”
